Column meanings, which need stating because two of them are easy to confuse:

* **enclosed** -- fraction of directions out of the ligand centre that run into peptide. Is the
  ligand in a shell, or against one face. 200 sampled directions, 3 A ray tolerance, 12 A reach.
* **wrapped** -- fraction of ligand heavy atoms with a peptide heavy atom within 4.5 A. Contact, not
  enclosure. **These disagree usefully**: `orig_control` contacts 75% of the ligand while enclosing
  only 49% of it, with the centroids 20.9 A apart -- the ligand is stuck to the outside.
* **centroid sep** -- ligand centroid to peptide centroid. Large against the peptide's own radius of
  gyration means the ligand is not inside it.
* **hints** -- forced contacts satisfied, of those requested. Uncorrelated with the rest: `esm1_f8`
  wraps 0.90 on 1 of 8, `orig_f4` wraps 0.85 on 3 of 4.

## The lesson to keep

**Measure the design objective, not a proxy for it.** The objective here is a peptide that *wraps*
the ligand. Hint satisfaction and interaction energy are proxies, and both inverted the verdict:

* the forced-contact experiment satisfied 3 of 4 at best and 0 of 12 at worst -- a near-total
  failure by its own constraint -- while raising ligand engagement from 75% to **100%**, the best
  result of anything tried;
* ESM2 substitution was indistinguishable from the original on summed energy (0.74 kcal/mol) while
  wrapping fell 75% -> 60% -> 40%.

Report ligand engagement beside any energy, and when a constraint-based experiment looks like a
failure, check the objective before concluding that it was one.

## About the method

1. **Two independently placed side chains essentially cannot be adjacent residues.** 0 successes in
   810 attempts at zero glycine spacers; still 2% at an indefensibly generous 1.0 A tolerance.
2. **Reachability is two-dimensional -- distance *and* approach angle.** At two spacers a partner
   approaching from behind its own fragment is reachable to 7.5 A; from the side, 11.1 A. Same
   linker cost, half again the distance. `build_sequence` sees neither, only centroid separation.
3. **A path needs a global linker budget, not a per-step one:** `sum(k) >= (3m-1)/2` for m segments.
   Surplus in one segment pays for a shortfall in the next. Reproduces every k = 0..4 closure rate
   measured, and both chain tests.
4. **The original designs were short of linkers by construction.** `RYGLGEFSDWKI` needs 13 spacers
   and has 2; **0 of its 9 steps are connectable as drawn**. This, not folding failure, is why the
   designed shell was not reproduced.
5. It also explains the earlier `--linker-slack 1` optimum: slack 1 takes the budget from 2 to 11
   against the 13 required, and that is where median displacement dropped 11.6 -> 6.8 A.
6. **The ordering search reaches all 12 poses of the richest shell** in a path where every step
   connects (132 of 132 pairs connectable): 33 residues, 21 spacers against a budget of 16.

## About Boltz contact constraints

7. **`force: false` is discarded, not softened** -- the featurizer skips it. Folds labelled "hinted"
   had nothing applied.
8. **`force: true` alone is conditioning**; the guidance that acts on the structure needs
   **`--use_potentials`**, off by default.
9. **Forced contacts do not reproduce the designed shell** -- best was 3 of 4, degrading to 0 of 12.
10. **But they dramatically improve ligand engulfment**, which is what the design is for. Fraction of
    the ligand's 20 heavy atoms engaged within 4.5 A: unconstrained controls 75% / 60% / 40%, forced
    folds up to **100%** (orig_f12 and esm2_f12), with orig_f12 making 47 contact pairs against the
    control's 27. **Judged by hint satisfaction the experiment looks like a failure; judged by
    wrapping it is the most effective intervention tried.**

## About the ESM2 linker substitution

11. **It locks the fold.** All four esm1 structures -- control and 4, 8, 12 forced contacts -- are
    the same fold to within **0.3-0.8 A** CA RMSD. ESM2 fills the linkers with alanine, leucine,
    lysine and glutamate, and the resulting rigid helix cannot be steered. The glycine-rich original
    moves 11.6-13.9 A under the same constraints, and is the only sequence that satisfied any.
12. **It degrades wrapping monotonically**: 75% engaged for the glycine design, 60% for esm1, 40%
    for esm2.
13. Energies alone do not show this: interaction -15.83 vs -12.22, ligand strain 9.14 vs 4.80, and
    the sums are within 0.74 kcal/mol. **Wrapping discriminates where net energy does not.**

## About measuring any of it

14. **No common binding mode exists across the twelve folds**: median 18.1 A CA RMSD superposed on
    the ligand, range 8.1-29.2.
15. **Hydrogen placement alone moved the interaction energy by 1.2 kcal/mol** (-14.47 vs -13.31 on
    one structure). pdbfixer is not deterministic and nothing relaxed the hydrogens. With
    hydrogen-only relaxation at fixed heavy atoms it reproduces to **0.08 kcal/mol**.
16. **Peptide strain is unusable in vacuum**: 237.8 kcal/mol and not converged in 75 steps. A
    relaxed peptide collapses into a compact ball, so the number measures collapse on the step
    budget, not strain. Needs solvent to mean anything.
17. **Ligand strain is real signal, not noise.** Between structures it varies by 4.34 kcal/mol
    (9.14 vs 4.80), far outside its 0.6 reproducibility -- the ligand adopts a different
    conformation for a different fold, and a fold that wraps harder makes it pay more.
18. **Explicit-water cavity desolvation is not affordable here**: ~416 atoms, 25 s per step, still
    descending 1.6 kcal/mol per step at step 102 of 200. One value obtained (control, 63.11 kcal/mol
    over 20 waters, truncated). Tens of kcal/mol, so not negligible -- but it should largely cancel
    between same-length designs, varying only with polar-residue distribution.
19. **The fragment library had two broken entries**, both written for this project rather than
    inherited: tyrosine was a C7H7 radical (59 electrons, odd, declared spin 1) plus a detached
    water and no phenol oxygen; glutamic was acetate, identical to aspartic. Now p-cresol and
    propionate, all ten entries distinct. Repairing them changed the selected shell from 10
    fragments to 8.

## 00:05 — Step 1 done: attachment geometry, in `code/attach_geom.py`

New module, no existing code changed. It perceives bonds from distances, finds each fragment's
capping carbon (a C with three H and exactly one heavy neighbour), tests whether several
candidates are equivalent, and returns the CA positions obtained by replacing each of that
carbon's hydrogens with a carbon at 1.53 A. `python code/attach_geom.py` prints the table;
`--pose <xyz> --frag <name>` does the same for a placed pose.

```
fragment       atoms formula    pieces     capping C    CA options  note
arginine          19 C4H12N3    [19]       [15]         [3]
lysine            17 C4H12N1    [17]       [12]         [3]
aspartic           7 C2H3O2     [7]        [2]          [3]
glutamic           7 C2H3O2     [7]        [3]          [3]
isoleucine        14 C4H10      [14]       [2, 9]       [3, 3]      candidates equivalent by symmetry
leucine           14 C4H10      [14]       [0, 5, 6]    [3, 3, 3]   candidates equivalent by symmetry
serine             6 C1H4O1     [6]        [2]          [3]
tryptophan        19 C9H9N1     [19]       [15]         [3]
tyrosine          17 C7H9O1     [14, 3]    [10]         [3]         NOT ONE MOLECULE: 2 pieces
phenylalanine     15 C7H8       [15]       [11]         [3]
```

**The ambiguity the plan flagged is not a chemistry call.** `NEXT_STEPS.md` said leucine's three
candidate methyls and isoleucine's two "must be resolved before either method can use them". They
resolve themselves: the leucine fragment is **isobutane** (C3v, three equivalent methyls) and the
isoleucine fragment is **n-butane** (C2, two equivalent methyls). The candidates are related by a
symmetry of the fragment, so the choice is free — any of them is the same molecule attached the
same way. Neither is the branched skeleton of the residue it is named for, but that only matters
if one insists on building the canonical residue; for condensation the fragment *is* the side chain.

### Two library defects found along the way

1. **The tyrosine fragment is a radical with the wrong spin, plus a loose water.** (First pass I
   called it "toluene plus a water"; on checking the valences it is worse than that.) The two
   pieces are:
   - a 14-atom piece of composition **C7H7**, in which ring carbon `C0` has **only two bonds**
     (`C1` and `C5`) and an open valence — a 4-methylphenyl radical, not toluene. Phenylalanine's
     fragment has 5 aromatic CH hydrogens; this one has 4.
   - a free **water** (`O15`–`H14`, `O15`–`H16`, both 0.96 A), the O sitting 3.10 A from `C0` —
     far too long for the phenol C–O bond that was clearly intended.

   The fragment dictionary declares `charge 0, spin 1`. **A C7H7 radical is a doublet.** Proved by
   electron count rather than by eye: the fragment has **59 electrons at charge 0**, which is odd,
   so a closed-shell singlet is impossible. It is the **only** entry in the library with an odd
   electron count -- every other fragment's declared `spin 1` is correct. So every
   tyrosine pose in `energies.csv` was scored by UMA as a closed-shell singlet when it is an
   open-shell radical, *and* with a spectator water in the system.

   `attach_geom.py` now checks this automatically and prints
   `WRONG SPIN: 59 electrons is odd, so this is a radical and needs spin 2, not 1`, and the strain
   code derives multiplicity from the electron count instead of hardcoding 1. Note this makes the
   new calculations **inconsistent with the old pose energies**, which used spin 1 -- correctly so,
   but the two should not be compared for tyrosine. The selected tyrosine pose
   (IE -7.2232 kcal/mol) carries both errors, and it is one of the ten fragments in every design.

   Note this is not repaired by `--drop-detached`: removing the water leaves the radical. Making it
   a real tyrosine needs the OH put back on `C0` (p-cresol); adding an H there instead would give
   toluene, i.e. a duplicate of phenylalanine. **That is a call for you, not me** — changing
   `define_fragments` would invalidate the 170 existing poses and all of `energies.csv`.
2. **`aspartic` and `glutamic` are the same molecule.** Both are acetate, `C2H3O2`, with identical
   sorted distance matrices — only the atom ordering differs (capping methyl at index 2 vs 3).

Net: the library's ten entries are **eight distinct chemistries** — propylguanidinium,
butylammonium, acetate (twice), n-butane, isobutane, methanol, 3-methylindole, toluene+water,
toluene. Worth knowing before any claim that the design explores ten side-chain types. Nothing
has been changed in `define_fragments`; the existing poses and energies stay exactly as they are.

## 00:20 — Step 2 built: `code/condense.py` and `code/condense_strain.py`

`condense.py` grows an ideal trans backbone (NeRF, omega fixed at 180) out of one placed
fragment's newly grafted CA and closes it onto the other's, over 2 + 2k free dihedrals for k
spacer glycines. `condense_strain.py` turns a closed solution into a complete molecule -- both
side chains exactly where the search put them, real backbone, neutral caps -- and relaxes it
twice with UMA: fragments fixed, then free. **strain = E_held - E_free**.

Everything geometric is validated: the builder reproduces every bond length, angle and dihedral
to 1e-6, omega comes out 180.0, closure is exactly 0 for a constructed-reachable target and
0.500 for a target moved 0.5 A. Built molecules come out as one connected component with correct
valences, and both CA centres are **L**, checked against a sign convention taken from the
Boltz-predicted peptides themselves (det[N-CA, C-CA, CB-CA] > 0 for all 10 of their residues,
and RDKit agrees via the CIP code).

### Two corrections I had to make to my own first attempt

1. **The closure condition was under-determined.** I first required only that residue B's amide
   nitrogen land on the circle of positions its fixed CA and CB allow -- 2 conditions. That
   leaves the amide angle C-N-CA free, and it obligingly collapsed: a built structure came out
   with C_1...CA_B at 1.98 A, i.e. an amide angle near 90 degrees. The condition is really
   **4**: the trans peptide unit places a CA from the last three backbone atoms, that position
   must *be* residue B's CA, and the N-CA-CB angle must be the residue's own. So k = 0 is
   generically unsolvable, k = 1 has isolated solutions, k >= 2 leaves a manifold. With 2
   conditions against 2 + 2k parameters, nearly everything "closed" -- the criterion was vacuous.
2. **The optimiser could return a self-clashing chain.** With no sterics in the objective, one
   solution put a spacer glycine's carbonyl carbon **0.31 A** from residue B's CA. There is now a
   steric term (backbone against both fragments, the ligand, and itself) and a `--steric` sweep
   mode. The terminal caps, which are arbitrary, get their orientation chosen by a scan instead
   of being nailed at 180 degrees, which had been burying a cap hydrogen 0.90 A inside a
   carboxylate oxygen.

Also: `np.cross` was 76% of the optimiser's runtime (it routes through `moveaxis`); an explicit
3-vector cross product made the objective 3.2x faster, 331 -> 105 us. The geometry checks above
were re-run afterwards and are still exact.

## 00:35 — why the designs never folded as drawn

`code/check_design.py` walks the design's *own* ordering and asks, step by step, whether the
backbone it prescribes can be built. It reproduces the pipeline's selection exactly -- the same
`RYGLGEFSDWKI` that is already in `runs/octinoxate/boltz/` -- so it is testing the real design.

The design assigns linker counts with `round(d_centroid / 3.8) - 1`, and for this design that is
**0 linkers on 7 of its 9 steps**. The sweep says k = 0 closed **0 times out of 32**. So almost
every step of the design asks for two independently placed fragments to sit at consecutive
residues, and the parameter count says why that essentially cannot happen: with CA and CB of
residue A both pinned by the pose, the only freedoms left are two dihedrals, against four
conditions to land residue B's CA where its pose demands. Two fragments placed independently
around a ligand are not going to satisfy that by luck.

The first step the sweep has covered so far, R3 -> Y1, is the pattern in miniature: assigned 0
linkers, closure 1.125 A, and it needs k = 1.

**The conclusion does not rest on the tolerance I chose.** A real peptide has a few degrees of
give in its bond angles, so "connected" needs a threshold, and one could reasonably argue for
anything from 0.1 to 0.5 A. Across the first 14 pairs the picture barely moves, and even at an
indefensibly generous 1.0 A, k = 0 connects 2% of the time:

| closure counted as connected | k=0 | k=1 | k=2 | k=3 | k=4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 A | 0.00 | 0.04 | 0.46 | 0.83 | 0.95 |
| 0.2 A | 0.00 | 0.04 | 0.52 | 0.85 | 0.95 |
| 0.5 A | 0.00 | 0.09 | 0.60 | 0.86 | 0.96 |
| 1.0 A | 0.02 | 0.22 | 0.69 | 0.89 | 0.96 |

That is a concrete, quantitative account of the 3.6% -- not "the folder failed to find the shell"
but "the shell was transcribed into a sequence that cannot hold it". Full per-step verdicts once
the sweep finishes. Note also that the linker formula measures **centroid to centroid**, while what
has to be bridged is **attachment point to attachment point**; for a fragment like tryptophan those
are several angstroms apart, so the formula is wrong in its reference points as well as blind to
direction.

A steric penalty of **30.9 A^2** -- by far the largest in the set -- costs 25.8 kcal/mol, while a
penalty of **0.0** costs 55.7. Whatever the penalty is measuring, it is not the energy.

**I generalised twice from too little data; both generalisations are dead.** First I said strain
tracks the steric penalty. Then, when that broke, I said it tracks the held-to-free RMSD. The
table above kills both:

- **penalty does not predict strain** -- arginine3 + aspartic21 has penalty **0.0** and costs
  55.7 kcal/mol, while arginine3 + lysine5 has penalty **12.1** and costs only 23.6;
- **RMSD does not predict it either** -- 1.43 A buys 254.5 kcal/mol in one case and 1.42 A buys
  55.7 in another.

The sober reading is that `E_held - E_free` is a **composite**, and at least four distinct things
are feeding it:

1. genuine backbone strain -- the thing we actually want;
2. **foregone salt bridges** -- both zero-penalty cases costing tens of kcal/mol are arginine
   against a carboxylate, held apart by the design and free to pair up on release;
3. **the loose water in the tyrosine fragment**, which migrates onto the arginine cation (below);
4. **geometric absurdity** -- the 254.5 outlier has its two CA positions 2.55 A apart, which is
   not a distance two alpha carbons can be, and that one really is a clash.

Note the arginine + lysine case cuts the other way: two cations that *repel*, so releasing them
buys little, and a big steric penalty still only costs 23.6 kcal/mol.

**What this means for using strain.** `E_held - E_free` is still meaningful -- it is the cost of
the designed arrangement relative to what the molecule would rather do, and a foregone salt bridge
*is* a genuine cost of a design. But it is **not comparable across pairs with different charge
states**, so it cannot be used to rank connections until the electrostatic part is separated out.
Until then, the only cases to trust as backbone strain are the ones with no charge pairing
available, no loose atoms and a small RMSD. The runner now records the closest approach between
the two side chains before and after release, which shows a salt bridge forming directly.

**This is the thing to fix before step 2 can be called finished.** A decomposition -- or simply a
charge-matched control set -- is what turns this from a number with caveats into a usable filter.

The clean case stands on its own merits: **arginine3 + serine31 costs 3.9 kcal/mol** with no charge
pairing available, no clash, exact closure, and a structure that barely moves when released
(0.25 A). That connection is real, and its structure is in `runs/octinoxate/condense/`.

### The tyrosine water is not a cosmetic defect

`arginine3 + tyrosine1`, k = 2: zero steric penalty, zero closure error, and **38.1 kcal/mol** of
"strain" with a 1.41 A relaxation. The tyrosine fragment is toluene **plus a loose water molecule**
(step 1). That water is frozen at its designed position during the constrained relaxation and free
to migrate during the unconstrained one, so it goes and solvates the arginine cation and donates
the entire energy of doing so straight into the strain. A guanidinium-water interaction in the gas
phase is worth tens of kcal/mol on its own, which is the whole number.

So the library defect found in step 1 corrupts any strain measurement involving tyrosine, and it
corrupted the fragment's original interaction energy in `energies.csv` too. `condense_strain.py`
now takes **`--drop-detached`**, which keeps only the atoms actually bonded to the capping carbon
(64 atoms -> 61 for this case, exactly the water). It is off by default so that nothing changes
silently, and `define_fragments` is untouched.

**Running tally of what inflates strain besides real backbone strain:** salt-bridge formation in
oppositely-charged pairs, and free-floating atoms in the fragment library. Both are visible in the
held-to-free RMSD, which is why that column is worth more than it looked.

Both closed geometrically. The second is nonetheless impossible: its two CA positions are 2.55 A
apart, which is not a distance two alpha carbons can be, and the backbone that formally connects
them has to thread through both side chains (steric penalty 12.8 A^2, worst remaining contact
2.23 A after relaxation). The energy says so without being told: **an order of magnitude and a
half between a connection that can be made and one that cannot.** That is the "where the strain
localises names the fragment pair" the plan was after, and it means strain can be used as a
filter, not just a description.

It also shows the geometric criterion alone is not sufficient -- closure 0.009 A on a physically
impossible arrangement -- which is what the steric term is for. The case picker has been fixed to
take the *smallest* k that connects rather than the best closure outright, which is what was
choosing k = 3 for a 2.55 A gap in the first place.

**The salt-bridge confound I flagged is real, and the RMSD column catches it.** The arginine +
glutamic case is +1 against -1, and its free relaxation moved 2.02 A RMSD -- it did not merely
relieve backbone strain, it went and formed the salt bridge. Some unknown part of that 81.6
kcal/mol is electrostatic reorganisation rather than the cost of holding the side chains in place.
Cases with a large held-to-free RMSD should not be read as pure backbone strain; the 21.3 kcal/mol

## 00:50 — a chain is strictly harder than a pair, and the sweep does not capture it

Writing step 4 (`code/condense_chain.py`) surfaced something that changes how the pairwise
criterion should be read.

When two fragments are condensed **on their own**, both ends are free: residue A's backbone can
rotate about its CA-CB bond, and so can residue B's. That is 2 + 2k free dihedrals against 4
closure conditions. **In a chain it is different.** Residue i's CA and CB are pinned by its pose,
and once the incoming nitrogen arrives from residue i-1, the outgoing carbonyl carbon has nowhere
left to go -- it is the one remaining tetrahedral slot, fixed by L chirality. So every segment
after the first has only **1 + 2k** free parameters against the same 4 conditions.

The consequence is sharp: in a chain, a segment with k = 1 has 3 parameters against 4 conditions
and is generically unsolvable, *even for a pair that connects perfectly well in isolation*. So

> **a chain's linker budget is global, not per-step** -- surplus in one segment pays for
> deficit in the next, and the pairwise sweep alone cannot tell you whether a path closes.

Any use of the pair criterion in method B has to carry that caveat, and the size of the gap is
now measured rather than assumed: `condense_chain.py` optimises every junction of a whole path at
once and reports the worst one. The builder is verified by round trip -- construct a chain from
known dihedrals, read off where the fragments must be, and it recovers closure ~1e-16 at every
junction.

### I got this wrong, and the test corrected it into something sharper

I predicted that a k = 1 segment would fail inside a chain even where the pair connects, because a
chain segment has 1 + 2k parameters rather than 2 + 2k. **The test refuted that.** Putting
`aspartic21 -> serine31` (which connects at k = 1 alone) as the second segment of
`arginine3 -> aspartic21 -> serine31` with k = [2, 1], the chain **closes at 0.054 A**.

The reason my per-segment count was wrong: surplus freedom does not stay in its own segment. The
first segment has more parameters than it needs, and the optimiser spends the excess steering where
its nitrogen arrives at the middle residue -- which is what sets that residue's outgoing carbonyl
direction, and therefore what the second segment has to work with. The count has to be **global**:

> parameters = `1 + m + 2*sum(k_i)` for m segments; conditions = `4m`.
> A path is generically closable when **`sum(k_i) >= (3m - 1) / 2`**.

That is a necessary condition from counting, not a sufficient one -- sterics and the actual
geometry still decide -- but it reproduces every measurement made tonight:

| case | m | sum k | parameters | conditions | predicted | observed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pair, k = 0 | 1 | 0 | 2 | 4 | infeasible | **0 of 127 close** |
| pair, k = 1 | 1 | 1 | 4 | 4 | exactly determined, marginal | 4% close |
| pair, k = 2 | 1 | 2 | 6 | 4 | 2 spare | 52% |
| pair, k = 3 | 1 | 3 | 8 | 4 | 4 spare | 85% |
| pair, k = 4 | 1 | 4 | 10 | 4 | 6 spare | 95% |
| chain, k = [2,2] | 2 | 4 | 11 | 8 | 3 spare | closes at 0.000, 6 of 27 CA choices |
| chain, k = [2,1] | 2 | 3 | 9 | 8 | 1 spare, marginal | closes at 0.054, 1 of 27 |

**What this says about the design.** `RYGLGEFSDWKI` chains 10 poses, so m = 9 and it needs
`sum(k) >= 13`. It has **2**. It is short by eleven residues of linker before geometry is even
consulted -- and this is a counting argument, independent of the tolerance debate entirely.

It also explains why `--linker-slack 1` helped in the earlier work (`README.md`: median
displacement 11.6 A -> 6.8 A): adding one glycine per gap takes sum(k) from 2 to 11, which is
close to the 13 the count demands. Slack 2 and 3 overshoot and the fold gets worse, which matches
the measured optimum at slack 1.

**For method B**, the edge-feasibility table from the pair sweep is still the right input, but the
path-level constraint is this global budget, not a per-edge test. `assign.py` now reports both: the
glycines a path's per-edge minimums supply, the budget the whole-path count demands, and the
shortfall.

## 01:35 — a note on the decomposition's arithmetic

Case 7 came back as `strain 23.63 = side chains -5.13 + backbone 28.75`. The **negative**
side-chain term is legitimate, not a bug: the free relaxation minimises the *whole* molecule, and
it will happily push the two side chains into a slightly worse mutual arrangement if the backbone
gains more than they lose. So the side chains can be more stable at the held geometry than at the
free one, and the backbone term then exceeds the total.

The property that *is* guaranteed is on the total: the free relaxation starts from the held
structure and only descends, so `strain = E_held - E_free >= 0` by construction. The components
carry no such constraint. Worth stating because a negative component looks like an error and is
not, and because it means the two terms should not be read as a partition into "good" and "bad"
parts -- they are a decomposition, and either can go either way.

## 01:45 — a negative "backbone strain" showed the decomposition was mislabelled

Case 9 came back `strain 64.44 = side chains 78.45 + backbone -14.01`. I said earlier that a
negative *side-chain* component is legitimate. A negative **backbone** term of -14 kcal/mol is
something else: it says the free structure's backbone is worse than the held one's, which happens
because the molecule **trades backbone quality for electrostatics** -- the release gains 78 kcal/mol
of salt bridge (the two side chains closing from 5.03 A to 2.67 A) and spends 14 of it distorting
the backbone to get there.

That is real behaviour, but it means the residual `total - sidechain` was not "backbone strain".
Two different things were being lumped into the side-chain term:

- how the two side chains **see each other** -- `E(A+B) - E(A) - E(B)`, where a salt bridge lives;
- each side chain's **own internal relaxation**, which involves neither the other side chain nor
  the backbone, and which was being charged to the backbone's account by omission.

Now split into three terms per case, six single points instead of two:

> `strain = sc_interaction + sc_internal + remainder`

and the last term is labelled **`remainder`**, not "backbone strain", because it still contains
backbone strain *and* backbone-to-side-chain coupling and I cannot separate those from total
energies alone. Honest naming matters here: I had been reporting a number as backbone strain that
was a residual with at least two other things in it.

the `+120 deg` one. Whenever chirality had actually sent it to `-120 deg`, HA was placed on top of
it. The `is_l_residue` guard never fired, because the carbon *was* correctly placed -- it was HA
that was wrong, and nothing was checking HA.

Fixed by measuring which slot the carbon occupies and putting HA in the other. Rebuilding to
confirm.

Worth noting what did **not** catch this: the closure criterion (0.006 A -- perfect), the steric
penalty (hydrogens are not in it), and the chirality check (which only looks at N, CA, C, CB).
Only counting bonds on every atom of the finished structure found it. That check is cheap and
should run on every build.

**One of the two flagged valences was a false positive of my own checker**: it treats a nitrogen
with four bonds as an error, but lysine's NZ is a protonated amine, `-NH3+`, and four is correct
for it. The checker, not the structure, is wrong there.

## 02:05 — the audit is now part of every build

`validate_structure()` in `condense_strain.py`: bond count per atom against what the element can
carry, connectivity, and a contact floor. Both the pair builder and the chain builder call it and
print any complaints, because the bug above got through closure, sterics and the chirality check
and only a bond count caught it.

It also fixes my earlier false positive: nitrogen is allowed 2, 3 **or 4** bonds, since lysine's
NZ is `-NH3+` and four is correct for it.

Run against the two existing chain builds it behaves as it should -- 1 complaint for the k = [2, 2]
build (the HA bug, now fixed in code) and 13 for the k = [1, 2] build that was made without sterics
and did not close.

## Boltz contact hints: three things learned by reading the code

The idea was to hand Boltz the designed shell as contact constraints, so the arrangement is asked
for instead of hoped for. Building it (`code/boltz_hints.py`) turned up:

1. **`force: false` is not a soft hint -- the constraint is discarded.** The featurizer does
   `if not force: continue`. Four folds labelled "hinted" had nothing applied at all; they were
   unhinted folds, which is why they matched the control.
2. **`force: true` alone is conditioning, not enforcement.** It enters the pair representation via
   `ContactConditioning`; the model is told and may decline.
3. **`--use_potentials` is what makes it bite.** A forced contact becomes a `ContactPotential`, but
   the guidance loop that applies potentials during sampling is gated on `fk_steering` /
   `physical_guidance_update`, both off by default.

Also found: **Boltz 2.2.1's affinity path is broken against RDKit 2026.03.6** -- it calls
`AllChem.Descriptors.MolWt`, and `AllChem` no longer exposes `Descriptors`. Requesting affinity
fails at parse time, so `boltz_check.py` as written would fail today. The workaround is on our side
(omit the affinity block); Boltz itself is untouched. The user notes the affinity head was unreliable
for these peptides anyway, so little is lost.

## Scoring: what the terms are, and two defects found in mine

`code/binding_energy.py`, with the explicit-water machinery copied from UMADock into
`code/solvate.py` and modified here (UMADock untouched):

* **interaction** -- as before
* **peptide and ligand strain**
* **cavity desolvation** -- the user's point: waters go **only where the ligand sits**, not around
  the whole peptide, because surrounding water is present before and after binding so it cancels,
  and including it would mean extracting a small cavity term as the difference of two large numbers
  with independent placement noise. Much cheaper too: ~245 atoms once instead of ~950, ~600 and ~90.

Four deviations from UMADock's water placement, all deliberate: water count scales with size
instead of being fixed at 20; rejection cutoff 2.4 A instead of 1.7 (1.7 A between two oxygens is a
clash); the rejection test covers all three atoms of the candidate water, not just its oxygen; and
the seed is settable.

**Defect 1: no progress visibility.** `BFGS(logfile=None)` threw away the optimiser log, so a
31-minute relaxation that had produced nothing looked identical to a stuck one. Now every relaxation
announces itself and streams its step table, and the CSV records steps taken and whether it
converged.

**Defect 2: hydrogens were never relaxed.** pdbfixer places rotatable hydrogens non-deterministically
and we went straight to a single point. The same structure scored **-14.47** and **-13.31 kcal/mol**
in two runs -- a 1.2 kcal/mol spread from hydrogen placement alone. Hydrogen-only relaxation with
heavy atoms fixed is now the default in both `binding_energy.py` and `uma_binding.py`: it removes
the arbitrariness while leaving the predicted heavy-atom geometry exactly as Boltz produced it.

Convergence is loose throughout on the user's argument that precision should not exceed the accuracy
of the method -- gas-phase MLIP, no continuum solvent, randomly placed cavity waters, one predicted
conformer. 0.10 eV/A over 75 steps for hydrogens and strain, 0.30 over 200 for the water shell. The
strain that implies is an **underestimate**, fine for comparing designs on one budget and not fine
quoted alone. (I had left the hydrogen relaxation at 0.05/200 by oversight; the user caught it.)

## Three arguments where the user corrected me

1. **Charged residues.** I treated a charge difference between designs as a confound. If a charged
   residue binds better that is the signal, and excluding it discards what we are trying to learn.
   What survives is narrower: the gas-phase salt bridge is *unscreened*, worth ~100 kcal/mol where
   in water it is single digits, so the term belongs in but is on a tenfold-inflated scale and
   cannot be summed naively with the others.
2. **Peptide size.** Same correction -- if a bigger peptide binds better that is signal. The
   specific reason for suspicion here is narrower: in this data the interaction energy does not
   track contact area (+0.17 against contact pairs, wrong sign), so whatever the size correlation
   picks up is not more-and-better contacts. Testable by asking whether the energy tracks contacts
   rather than length, which needs designs of different length folded the same way.
3. **The free peptide's salt bridge.** I called it a contamination of the strain number. It is a
   real competing state that a real peptide does form, and it costs real energy to open. Only my
   label was wrong, and the decomposition already puts it where it belongs.
